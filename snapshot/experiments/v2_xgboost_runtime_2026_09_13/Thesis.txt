Chapter 1
Introduction
1.1
Motivation
Safe driving in urban environments requires more than detecting a pedestrian and es-
timating their current position. A vehicle must also anticipate what that pedestrian
may do next. A pedestrian waiting beside the road may remain stationary, continue
parallel to the curb, or begin crossing. These alternatives can look similar at one in-
stant, yet they require different responses from an intelligent vehicle. A useful prediction
must therefore be made before the crossing action becomes obvious, while sufficient time
remains for planning and comfortable intervention.
Pedestrian crossing prediction is difficult because the available evidence is partial and
time-dependent. Bounding-box motion can reveal displacement and scale change, but it
cannot describe what occurs inside the box. Appearance and scene context can reveal
the curb, road, traffic controls, and nearby vehicles, but they are high-dimensional and
can encode correlations specific to one dataset. Ego-motion affects apparent pedestrian
motion and helps interpret these observations. Human pose occupies an intermediate
position: it can be estimated automatically from images and can expose head, torso, arm,
and leg configuration, but its keypoints are noisy, view-dependent, and not themselves
an explanation of behavior.
The central problem of this thesis is how to use that pose evidence in a form that
is both useful for future crossing prediction and inspectable by a human analyst. A
high-capacity model can learn a latent pose representation, but it may be difficult to
determine whether a decision depended on stride, torso orientation, foot movement,
or an unintended correlation.
Conversely, a compact set of named measurements is
easier to inspect but may discard information or amplify pose-estimation errors. The

scientific question is therefore not simply whether pose can be added to a classifier. It
is which pose-derived measurements are useful, when they become useful, and how they
complement location, motion and context. Changes in posture, orientation, gait and
body motion may reveal preparation before a large displacement becomes visible; their
anticipatory value must be tested under a future-crossing protocol.
The problem is studied using the Pedestrian Intention Estimation (PIE) and Joint At-
tention in Autonomous Driving (JAAD) datasets [1], [2].
PIE provides the primary
empirical basis; JAAD supplies secondary in-domain evaluations.
1.2
Anticipatory Crossing Prediction
This thesis addresses binary future crossing prediction from an onboard monocular ob-
servation. The target is an observable future event: whether the pedestrian will cross,
not an unverifiable claim about the person’s private mental state.
This operational
definition separates the task from several adjacent problems.
Pedestrian detection identifies where a person appears in an image, and tracking links
that person across frames. Current-action recognition determines whether crossing is
already occurring. Trajectory forecasting estimates future positions, which may indi-
rectly reflect crossing but has a different output and loss. Risk assessment combines
the predicted states of multiple road users with vehicle dynamics. Crossing anticipa-
tion depends on all these neighboring capabilities, but it asks a narrower question at a
deliberately earlier time: what future crossing event is supported by the observations
available before the event begins?
The main benchmark uses 16 observed frames and a time-to-event (TTE) interval of
30–60 frames [3]. Chapter 2 explains this temporal convention; Chapter 3 specifies the
exact sample construction and evaluation rules.
1.3
Research Context and Problem Development
Prior coauthored work on MSLSTM-PID provides the research lineage for studying how
pedestrian, motion, vehicle and scene inputs can be combined in a crossing predictor
[4]. The present thesis develops that interest through explicit pose measurements and
a lightweight prediction architecture. Adapted MSLSTM and PedCMT models [5] pro-
vide supporting tests of representation complementarity on the 16-frame/TTE 30–60
benchmark. Their actual inputs and training procedures are documented in Chapter 3;
the thesis does not depend on reproducing published headline scores.

The resulting representation is called the Behavioral Intention Unit (BIU). A BIU is
a named geometric measurement derived from estimated two-dimensional pose and in-
tended to describe visible posture, orientation, or movement. Its organization is concep-
tually inspired by the Facial Action Coding System (FACS) [6]. Chapter 2 develops that
limited analogy and the geometric precedents. The contribution lies in the particular
crossing-oriented representation and its organization.
BIU version 1 establishes the initial cue set and comparison framework. BIU version 2
adds direction-aware refinements and supplies the main 56-dimensional representation.
Their development is retained explicitly, with version comparisons interpreted for the
predictor and metric being studied. The methodology documents their ViTPose-family
sources [7], normalization and direction-handling rules.
Bounding boxes remain central throughout this development. They are inexpensive,
widely available, and can encode strong trajectory and scale information.
BIUs are

This controlled comparison also prevents a
pose method from receiving credit for motion information that a strong box model

A major part of the work is the architecture that combines BIUs with bounding-box
dynamics, temporal summaries, ego-motion and optional inferred road context using
XGBoost as the crossing predictor [8]. BIU equations deterministically construct the
measurements; XGBoost learns their predictive interactions from labeled training win-
dows. This is a non-neural crossing predictor with pretrained neural perception up-
stream. The evaluation distinguishes measured downstream efficiency from the cost of
pose estimation and road perception.
1.4
Problem Statement
The problem is to organize pose evidence into measurements with explicit geometric
meanings and integrate them with motion and context in an effective, computationally
economical crossing predictor. Defining plausible cues alone does not establish their
value: their contribution must be assessed against strong baseline and raw-pose inputs
under matched conditions.
The thesis therefore connects representation design with architecture and evaluation. It
asks whether the same measurements help different predictors, when their value changes
across datasets, and what accuracy and computational costs follow from the design.

1.7
Scope and Limitations
The thesis is scoped to binary crossing prediction from monocular onboard observations.
It does not propose a complete autonomous-driving stack and does not solve pedestrian
detection, tracking, three-dimensional pose estimation, trajectory planning, or vehicle
control. Bounding boxes and estimated keypoints are treated as upstream inputs whose
source and quality must be reported.
The main temporal setting belongs to the 16-observation-frame, TTE 30–60 benchmark
family. The separate PSI supporting study retains its own perceived-intention protocol.
PIE provides the primary published-work comparison.
JAAD results apply only to
the declared subset and event construction, with mixed outcomes assessed at the fixed
operating threshold. These separately trained models do not establish zero-shot transfer
of the main BIU-v2 predictor.

secondary evidence; they do not establish equivalence to deployed Action/Look detec-
tors. Training such detectors is outside the thesis scope.

Background and Related Work
2.1
Pedestrian Crossing Intention Prediction
Chapter 1 defined the anticipatory crossing task and distinguished it from detection,
tracking and current-action recognition. This chapter examines how previous work for-
mulates that task, represents the available evidence and evaluates predictions.
The literature uses several closely related terms, including pedestrian intention predic-
tion, pedestrian action prediction, crossing action anticipation and crossing behavior
prediction. They may refer to different targets: a semantically defined intention or an
observable crossing action within a future interval. Comparisons in this review therefore
attend to the operational target as well as the method’s name.
The first study accompanying the Joint Attention in Autonomous Driving (JAAD)
dataset framed pedestrian behavior understanding as a combination of visual, behav-
ioral, and contextual analysis. It introduced annotations not only for pedestrian loca-
tions, but also for actions, attention-related behavior, driver behavior, and properties of
the traffic scene [2]. The Pedestrian Intention Estimation (PIE) dataset later extended
this line of work with continuous onboard footage, richer spatial annotations, and ego-
vehicle measurements [1]. Together, the two datasets shifted the research problem away
from isolated pedestrian appearance and toward the interaction between the pedestrian,
the approaching vehicle, and the road environment.

2.2
Temporal Formulation of the Prediction Task
2.2.1
Observation windows, events, and time-to-event
Let a pedestrian track contain observations indexed by frame. For a window ending at
frame t, the model receives a sequence
Ot = {xt−T+1, . . . , xt},
where T is the observation length and xk contains the selected features at frame k. The
target is defined relative to an event frame e, usually the first frame in which a crossing
pedestrian is annotated as crossing. The time-to-event (TTE) is
TTE = e −t.
For a genuine anticipation sample, t < e.
The observation ends before the crossing
begins, and the model predicts whether the future event belongs to the crossing or
non-crossing class.
The benchmark proposed by Kotseruba, Rasouli, and Tsotsos standardizes the com-
monly used setting to an observation of 16 frames, approximately half a second at 30
frames per second, with the last observation frame located between 30 and 60 frames
before the event [3]. The resulting TTE range is therefore one to two seconds. Pos-
itive samples are aligned to the beginning of the crossing action. For a non-crossing
track, where no crossing onset exists, the last observable frame is used as the reference
event. This construction gives crossing and non-crossing tracks a comparable temporal
endpoint while ensuring that positive observations occur before the action.
The choice of a 16-frame window is not a claim that half a second is universally optimal.
It is a benchmark convention that allows models to be compared under the same amount
of recent history. A longer observation may expose more gradual changes, but it may
also include irrelevant earlier behavior and reduce the number of usable tracks. A shorter
observation may respond quickly to a sudden cue but provide less temporal evidence.
The same applies to the prediction horizon. Near-event predictions are easier because
the body and trajectory often begin to change visibly. Farther horizons are more useful
for planning but are less certain. For this reason, the observation length and TTE must
be reported together with every result.

2.2.2
Anticipation versus current-action classification
A recurring problem in the literature is the use of the term prediction for settings where
the observation includes frames after the crossing has started.
Once a pedestrian is
already in the road, the task becomes current-action recognition rather than anticipation.
Such samples are not useless: an autonomous system still needs to detect and track an
ongoing crossing. However, mixing them with pre-event samples changes the meaning
of the reported performance.
This issue was one of the motivations for the common benchmark. Earlier studies differed
in whether they sampled only pre-event frames, the entire pedestrian track, or a mixture
that extended beyond the event. The benchmark analysis showed that methods become
progressively more accurate as the observation approaches or passes the crossing point
[3]. Consequently, a high average score can hide weak performance in the period where
an early decision is actually required.
2.2.3
Window overlap and sampling density
A single track can generate more than one observation window. If a window is moved by
a small stride, successive samples share most of their frames. The overlap ratio controls
this sampling density. The benchmark uses an overlap of 0.6 for PIE and 0.8 for JAAD
[3]. The higher overlap in JAAD compensates partly for its shorter tracks and smaller
behavior-annotated subset.
Overlap increases the number of training samples, but it does not create the same
amount of independent information. Two windows from the same pedestrian may differ
by only a few frames, and they share the same scene, identity, clothing, and eventual
event. This matters for both data splitting and statistical analysis. Windows belonging
to the same track must not be divided across training and test sets. It also means that
a test set containing thousands of windows may represent only hundreds of independent
pedestrian tracks.
The sampling protocol further depends on how positive and negative tracks are defined.
A positive track can be aligned to a single clean crossing onset, but negative tracks
can include several distinct situations: a person waiting at the curb, walking parallel
to the road, moving away, or merely appearing far from the roadway. Combining these
cases without documenting the selection rule changes the difficulty of the task. The
label definition, event anchor, overlap, and stride are therefore part of the experimental
method, not implementation details that can be omitted.

2.2.4
Horizon-dependent evidence
The evidence available for crossing prediction can change with the prediction horizon.
Preparatory orientation and trajectory cues may be visible earlier, whereas foot lift,
stride development, and other gait changes may become clearer closer to the event. A
broad TTE interval can therefore combine observation windows that contain different
stages of behavior.
Reporting performance across TTE intervals helps determine when a representation con-
tributes and whether different modalities provide complementary evidence at different
stages. Horizon analysis is consequently part of both task formulation and interpreta-
tion, rather than only an additional performance breakdown.
2.3
Datasets for Pedestrian Intention Prediction
2.3.1
Joint Attention in Autonomous Driving
The Joint Attention in Autonomous Driving dataset, commonly referred to as JAAD,
was introduced to support the study of pedestrian behavior in urban traffic scenes [2].
The original release contains more than 300 short video clips collected with vehicle-
mounted monocular cameras in North America and Europe. The videos include variation
in camera properties, road layout, weather, visibility, and time of day. This variation is
one of JAAD’s main strengths: it exposes models to more heterogeneous scenes than a
dataset collected in one location under one weather condition.
JAAD provides pedestrian bounding boxes with track identities and occlusion informa-
tion. A behavior-annotated subset includes labels describing pedestrian actions before
and during crossing, attention-related actions such as looking or glancing, responses to
the approaching vehicle, and demographic attributes. Scene-level context includes road
configuration, traffic controls, weather, and time of day. Driver actions are also anno-
tated qualitatively. These labels made JAAD one of the first large pedestrian datasets
intended for behavior analysis rather than detection alone.
The richness of JAAD comes with an important structural distinction. Not every tracked
person has the same behavioral annotation coverage. The benchmark literature therefore
distinguishes between the behavior-annotated subset, commonly called JAADbeh, and
a broader collection that includes other visible pedestrians, commonly called JAADall
[3]. The broader collection adds many pedestrians who are not near a crossing decision,
while the behavior subset is concentrated around crossing interactions. These subsets

Chapter 2 Background and Related Work
11
differ in class balance, context, pedestrian scale, and label availability. A result reported
on JAADbeh cannot be treated as directly equivalent to a result on JAADall or on a
stricter event-filtered subset.
JAAD also lacks the continuous numeric ego-vehicle sensor measurements available in
PIE. Some benchmark implementations use qualitative vehicle-action labels, while others
omit ego-motion from JAAD experiments. This difference affects multimodal methods
that rely strongly on speed. Pose keypoints are not supplied as ground-truth joint anno-
tations; they are normally generated using an external pose estimator. Any pose-based
evaluation on JAAD therefore includes the errors and failure modes of that estimator.
For this thesis, JAAD is valuable as a challenging cross-dataset and robustness setting.
Its shorter tracks, wider variation in scene conditions, and smaller pedestrian scales make
it different from PIE. At the same time, care is needed when constructing event-aligned
labels because the available annotation types differ across pedestrian categories. The
exact subset used in each experiment is therefore stated explicitly rather than being
described only as “JAAD.”
2.3.2
Pedestrian Intention Estimation
The Pedestrian Intention Estimation dataset, or PIE, was designed specifically for in-
tention estimation and first-person pedestrian trajectory prediction [1]. It contains more
than six hours of continuous driving footage recorded in downtown Toronto with a cal-
ibrated monocular dashboard camera at 30 frames per second. The footage is divided
into approximately ten-minute videos grouped into six sets. Unlike JAAD’s geographi-
cally varied short clips, PIE was recorded in daylight under clear or overcast conditions
in a single city. Its strength lies instead in continuity, longer pedestrian tracks, and
richer ego-vehicle and spatial annotations.
PIE includes 1,842 behavior-annotated pedestrian samples.
The original release re-
ports approximately 911,000 video frames, 293,000 annotated frames, and about 740,000
pedestrian bounding boxes. Each pedestrian of interest is assigned a track identity and
annotated with bounding boxes, occlusion states, crossing-related actions, looking be-
havior, and other attributes. Spatial annotations cover relevant road objects and in-
frastructure, while the vehicle platform provides information such as speed, GPS, and
heading. The dataset also includes human-reference intention scores obtained through
controlled and online studies, reflecting how human observers interpreted whether a
pedestrian wanted to cross.

For benchmarked crossing-action prediction, the most important property of PIE is its
canonical set-level split. The benchmark uses set01, set02, and set06 for training, set04
and set05 for validation, and set03 for testing [3]. This split separates complete video
sets rather than randomly dividing overlapping windows. It reduces the risk that nearly
identical scenes or pedestrians appear in both training and test data.
PIE is particularly suitable for studying geometry.
The continuous tracks allow the
construction of bounding-box motion features, while the calibrated onboard setting and
ego-speed measurements support analyses of relative motion. Its annotations also make
it possible to align observation windows to crossing onset.
However, PIE does not
provide ground-truth anatomical keypoints for all pedestrians. As with JAAD, pose-
based studies normally run a pretrained pose estimator on the pedestrian crops or boxes.
This distinction is important: the pedestrian and crossing labels may be ground truth,
but the pose measurements are estimated.
PIE and JAAD should not be viewed simply as a large dataset and a small dataset. Their
sampling conditions, annotation coverage, scene diversity, track lengths, and available
vehicle information differ substantially. A representation that works well on PIE may
rely on smooth scale and trajectory patterns that transfer poorly to JAAD. Conversely, a
model trained to exploit JAAD’s varied context may not benefit equally from PIE’s more
regular recording conditions. Evaluation on both datasets is useful precisely because they
expose different weaknesses.

2.4
Information Sources for Crossing Prediction
Crossing prediction methods can be organized by architecture, but architecture alone
does not explain what a model is able to observe.
A recurrent network using only
bounding boxes and a transformer using the same boxes receive similar physical evidence,
even if they encode it differently. It is therefore useful to first review the main information
sources.
2.4.1
Bounding boxes and image-plane trajectories
Bounding boxes are the most widely available representation in pedestrian datasets and
deployed perception systems. A box records the image-plane extent of a pedestrian,
usually through two corners or through center coordinates, width, and height. Across
time, these values provide a compact description of apparent motion and scale.
Several useful signals can be derived from a box sequence. Changes in the center co-
ordinates describe lateral and vertical image motion. Width and height changes reflect
apparent scale, which is influenced by the relative motion between the pedestrian and the

Chapter 2 Background and Related Work
14
camera. Displacement, velocity, acceleration, aspect ratio, and area change can be com-
puted without inspecting the pedestrian’s internal appearance. These properties make
bounding boxes computationally attractive and relatively robust when the pedestrian is
small.
Bounding-box dynamics can be surprisingly predictive. A person who is approaching
the road, moving laterally across the image, or changing scale in a regular way leaves a
clear geometric trace. The PIE work showed that past location and local context pro-
vide complementary information for intention estimation, and that ego-vehicle dynam-
ics improve first-person trajectory prediction [1]. Later trajectory and action-prediction
models continued to use boxes as either a baseline or a major modality.
The limitation is equally clear. A rectangle does not explicitly describe whether the
pedestrian is turning their head, shifting weight, lifting a foot, changing stride, or ro-
tating the torso. Different body states can produce nearly identical boxes. A person
standing at the curb and a person beginning to step forward may have similar center
and scale values over a short window. Bounding boxes describe where the pedestrian is
and how the enclosing region changes, not what the body is doing inside that region.
This limitation does not make boxes a weak baseline. On the contrary, it makes them an
essential baseline for pose research. If a pose-derived representation is evaluated without
a strong box model, improvements may simply reflect motion and scale information that
the box already contained. The relevant question is not whether pose can predict crossing
in isolation, but what pose adds beyond trajectory geometry.
2.4.2
Appearance and local visual context
Appearance-based approaches use cropped pedestrian images, enlarged local regions, or
full video frames. These inputs preserve clothing, body shape, visible limbs, surrounding
curb structure, and nearby objects. Convolutional and video models can learn features
directly from these pixels without requiring an explicit pose representation.
The original JAAD baselines analyzed pedestrian gait and attention-related cues from
image crops and combined them with scene context [2]. PIE later compared tight pedes-
trian crops with wider local-context crops and found that location and context could
complement one another [1]. Spatio-temporal DenseNet approaches, recurrent visual en-
coders, and 3D convolutional networks extended the same idea to short image sequences.
The advantage of local context is that it preserves evidence that boxes and skeletons
omit. A crop can show whether the pedestrian is near a curb, partly occluded by a
parked vehicle, or facing an open road. The disadvantage is that the representation

Chapter 2 Background and Related Work
15
is high-dimensional and often difficult to interpret.
It also depends on crop design.
A tight crop may exclude the road, while a large crop may introduce unrelated back-
ground and neighboring pedestrians. These crop-design trade-offs recur in recurrent,
attention-based, and segmentation-assisted visual-context models [3], [12], [13]. Because
pixel inputs retain background appearance, learned visual representations may also en-
code dataset-specific texture or location patterns, which is a concern when models are
transferred across recording domains [3].
Some methods use a predicted future trajectory as an intermediate representation. Tra-
jFusionNet, for example, predicts pedestrian trajectories and vehicle speed and supplies
both a sequential representation and a visual overlay of the predicted boxes to its in-
tention classifier [14]. This makes the spatial forecast explicit, but it also introduces an
upstream prediction stage whose errors can affect the final decision. Other methods use
optical flow or related motion-flow representations, computed either around the pedes-
trian or over a wider scene, to expose temporal image motion [3], [9], [15], [16]. Such
flow contains pedestrian, background, and camera motion; estimating it adds compu-
tation beyond box differencing, and its reliability around small or occluded pedestrians
depends on the upstream flow estimator.
2.4.3
Environmental and semantic context
A pedestrian’s crossing decision is constrained by the road scene.
Relevant factors
include the presence and type of crosswalk, curb position, number of lanes, traffic signals,
parked vehicles, road boundaries, and the behavior of other road users. Context can be
supplied as human-written labels, semantic segmentation maps, object detections, scene
graphs, or features learned from full images.
The form of context matters. Human-provided labels such as “at crosswalk,” “traffic
light present,” or “looking” are clean and compact, but they are not directly available
in deployment. A real system must infer them through perception models, and those
errors propagate into the predictor. Segmentation maps and object detections are closer
to a deployable pipeline, but they increase computational cost and may fail under poor
visibility. Raw full-frame features avoid choosing a fixed ontology, but they offer less
control over what the model actually uses.
Yang et al. combined local pedestrian appearance, semantic global context, bounding
boxes, pose, and ego-vehicle speed through recurrent encoders and attention-based fu-
sion [17]. CIPF later organized eight visual and non-visual inputs into observational,
contextual, and convolutional fusion modules [18]. PIP-Net added categorical depth and
multi-camera context to better represent road-user proximity and scene dynamics [9].

16
Context-Ped instead used pedestrian crops and inferred road-structure segmentation to
study a smaller visual-only input set [13]. These methods illustrate the performance
potential of context, but they also show why input assumptions must be reported. A
model using dataset annotations is solving a different upstream perception problem from
a model that derives context from raw video.
2.4.4
Ego-vehicle dynamics
From an onboard camera, pedestrian motion is entangled with camera motion. A sta-
tionary pedestrian can change position and scale in the image because the vehicle is
moving.
The same pedestrian behavior also has different implications depending on
whether the vehicle is stopped, approaching quickly, or decelerating.
PIE provides numeric ego-vehicle measurements, making speed a common input to mod-
els evaluated on that dataset. SF-GRU, PCPA, IntFormer, feature-fusion models, and
later multimodal systems include ego-speed or vehicle state alongside pedestrian fea-
tures. IntFormer’s ablation study identified ego-vehicle speed as particularly influential
in its PIE experiments [19]. First-person trajectory models similarly show that vehicle
speed helps separate apparent image motion from pedestrian motion [20], [21].
Ego-speed should therefore be treated as a genuine modality rather than a minor aux-
iliary variable. At the same time, models that use it are not directly comparable with
models that do not, especially on datasets where the signal is unavailable or represented
only by qualitative labels. This thesis studies pose and bounding-box representations
without assuming that every benchmark supplies the same vehicle information.
2.4.5
Human pose
Two-dimensional human pose represents a pedestrian as a set of anatomical keypoints,
typically including the head, shoulders, elbows, wrists, hips, knees, and ankles. It oc-
cupies a middle ground between bounding boxes and raw appearance. Pose is more
structured than pixels and more descriptive of body configuration than a rectangle.
Crossing-related pose evidence can arise from gait, weight transfer, head orientation,
torso lean, shoulder rotation, arm motion, and foot placement. Early work showed that
engineered 2D pose features could support crossing classification with shallow models
[22], [23]. Later methods used complete skeleton sequences, temporal convolution, re-
current units, or graph convolution to learn pose representations automatically [24]–[26].
Recent models have again combined learned skeleton dynamics with explicit orientation

Chapter 2 Background and Related Work
17
or position transformations [27], [28], making the distinction between named geometry
and latent pose encoding especially relevant.
Pose is not a direct measurement of intention. It is an estimate of visible body config-
uration in the image plane. A head-direction cue does not prove that the pedestrian
has seen the vehicle. A lifted foot may indicate normal walking parallel to the road
rather than stepping into it. The meaning emerges from temporal change and from the
relation between pose, location, and context. This is why pose should be studied as
complementary evidence rather than as a complete replacement for other modalities.
2.5
Model Families in Pedestrian Intention Prediction
2.5.1
Engineered features and shallow classifiers
Before large multimodal neural networks became common, crossing prediction was often
approached through explicit motion, context, and body features. Keller and Gavrila
studied whether a pedestrian would cross using motion and path-related information
[15].
Schneemann and Heinemann incorporated contextual cues for urban crossing-
intention detection [29]. These methods reflected a practical assumption: if the relevant
variables can be defined clearly, a comparatively simple classifier may be sufficient.
Pose-based work followed the same principle. Fang, Vazquez, and L´opez proposed an
onboard intention-detection approach using estimated pose and explicit pedestrian fea-
tures [22]. Fang and L´opez then examined crossing prediction from 2D pose using a
shallow classifier [23]. Quintero and colleagues combined pedestrian path, pose, activity
recognition, and Gaussian-process dynamical modeling [30]. The significance of these
studies is not only historical. They show that body geometry can be translated into
semantically meaningful measurements instead of being passed to an opaque end-to-end
model.
Engineered features make the variables supplied to a classifier explicit. If stride, torso
orientation, or distance to curb is used, the researcher can inspect its definition and
contribution. Compact measurements can also reduce downstream processing, although
data requirements and computational savings depend on the feature extractor, classifier,
and evaluation setting and must be measured.
Several classifier families can learn from such tabular measurements. Logistic regression
(LR) models the log-odds of the positive class as a linear function of the supplied features
and converts that score to a probability through a sigmoid [31]. It provides a linear

Chapter 2 Background and Related Work
18
reference: nonlinear geometric relationships can be encoded in the input features, while
the classifier itself learns their weighted combination.
A random forest (RF) aggregates randomized decision trees, commonly trained on boot-
strap samples with random feature subsets considered at each split [32]. Each tree par-
titions the feature space through threshold decisions. Aggregation reduces reliance on
any single tree, while successive splits can express nonlinear relationships between cues.
Gradient boosting builds an additive predictor sequentially, with each new tree fitted
using the current ensemble’s prediction errors. XGBoost implements regularized tree
boosting using first- and second-order loss information to construct trees and estimate
their leaf scores [8]. LightGBM is another gradient-boosted tree system, designed for
efficient learning from large feature tables [33]. Both learn decision rules from labels,
even when the input measurements are deterministic. These four classifiers supply the
linear, randomized-tree and boosted-tree baselines used in the thesis; their experiment-
specific preprocessing and training settings are given in Chapter 3.
The fit between a representation and a predictor also matters. Grinsztajn et al. found
strong tree-model performance on their medium-sized tabular benchmarks and investi-
gated differences in sensitivity to uninformative features, feature orientation and irregu-
lar target functions [34]. Those findings motivate examining tree ensembles for explicit
geometric measurements, but they do not establish a universal advantage over neural
networks or determine the outcome of a pedestrian-crossing benchmark.
Recent work also combines neural perception with a non-neural crossing predictor.
PCAP-SL uses frozen vision-transformer features from pedestrian crops, OpenPose coor-
dinates, and ego-speed where available, with a sequence of XGBoost classifiers carrying
prediction information across observation steps [35]. Here XGBoost is a learned ensem-
ble of decision trees; a fixed feature-extraction procedure does not make the crossing
decision a hand-written rule. PCAP-SL is relevant to the architecture studied in this
thesis because it separates upstream neural feature extraction from downstream tree-
based prediction. Its crop embeddings and sequential learning procedure differ from the
proposed pose-derived architecture defined in Chapter 3. Efficiency comparisons must
specify whether they include upstream perception or only the crossing predictor.
The same explicitness is a limitation. A manually chosen feature can miss an impor-
tant pattern or encode a camera-specific bias. Thresholded rules may be unstable when
keypoints are noisy. Features that appear intuitive may be redundant with one another,
and a useful cue at one TTE may carry little information at another. For these rea-
sons, engineered representations should not be justified only by intuition. They need

controlled ablations against raw geometry, evaluation across datasets, and analysis of
when their signal appears.
2.5.2
Recurrent and multimodal architectures
Pedestrian crossing prediction is based on short sequences, which made recurrent neural
networks a natural early choice. Long short-term memory (LSTM) and gated recurrent
unit (GRU) models encode a sequence one step at a time, maintaining a hidden state
intended to summarize previous observations.
This allowed models to move beyond
static images and capture changes in motion, posture, and context.
SF-GRU introduced a stacked recurrent architecture that fused pedestrian appearance,
local context, pose, bounding boxes, and ego-vehicle speed at different levels [12]. The
benchmark study later evaluated recurrent baselines and proposed PCPA, which used
attention to combine image, pose, box, and vehicle features [3]. These models helped
establish multimodal recurrent fusion as a strong baseline family.
A central design choice is where fusion occurs. Early fusion concatenates features before
temporal encoding. It is simple, but modalities with different scales and noise char-
acteristics are mixed immediately. Late fusion encodes each modality separately and
combines compact representations near the classifier. This supports specialization but
may miss fine-grained interactions between streams. Hierarchical and hybrid fusion at-
tempt to capture both, combining related inputs at intermediate stages and applying
attention to the fused representation.
Yang et al. systematically compared feature-fusion strategies and used separate visual
and non-visual pathways with recurrent encoding and attention [17]. CIPF grouped eight
modalities according to how they should be processed, rather than forcing every input
through the same encoder [18]. PIP-Net combined kinematic features with contextual
depth, semantic information, and temporal attention, and also explored a wider multi-
camera field of view [9]. SafeCrossNet subsequently combined scene-interaction features
with vehicle-side non-visual dynamics through hierarchical spatio-temporal fusion [36].
These studies support a consistent conclusion: the value of a modality depends not only
on whether it is included, but on how it is represented and fused.
Multimodal models can achieve strong performance because they reduce ambiguity. A
box trajectory can be interpreted together with a pedestrian crop, pose, road layout,
and vehicle speed. Their main drawback is that they can blur the source of the decision.
A model may report that attention favored one stream, but this does not necessarily
explain which concrete behavior inside that stream was decisive.
They also depend

Chapter 2 Background and Related Work
20
on the availability and quality of several upstream inputs. A failure in segmentation,
tracking, pose, or vehicle telemetry can alter the fused representation in ways that are
difficult to diagnose.
MSLSTM-PID is a multi-stream recurrent model for PIE, with published inputs covering
pose, motion, annotated pedestrian behavior, ego-vehicle dynamics and scene informa-
tion [4]. The thesis adapts this architecture to 16 observed frames and TTE 30–60; its
actual stream configurations and training are specified in Chapter 3.
2.5.3
Transformer and attention-based methods
Transformers offered an alternative to recurrent sequence processing by allowing the
model to relate every observation step to every other step through self-attention. This
can be useful when the decisive cue is not confined to the final frame. A head turn early
in the observation or a brief pause can influence the interpretation of later motion.
IntFormer combined a video encoder with a transformer-based encoder for bounding
boxes, pose, and ego-speed [19]. It followed the benchmark task and emphasized effi-
ciency as well as feature selection. CAPformer extended this direction with transformer-
based video and kinematic branches, showing that self-attention could provide a practical
alternative to recurrent architectures [37]. PIT used progressive interaction modeling to
capture relations among modalities and over time [38].
Transformer models are often described as better at long-range dependencies, but this
advantage should be interpreted in relation to the task. The benchmark observation
contains only 16 frames. The question is therefore not whether the network can remem-
ber a very long sequence, but whether attention provides a useful way to select moments
and interactions inside a short, heterogeneous observation. The architecture may also
increase parameter count and training requirements, depending on the visual backbone
and number of streams.
Attention weights are sometimes presented as explanations.
They can show that a
model assigned more weight to a frame, feature token, or modality, but they do not
by themselves establish why that input caused the prediction. An attended pose token
remains a high-dimensional representation. This motivates a distinction used throughout
this thesis: attention can improve selective processing, while interpretability requires
that the underlying evidence itself can be named and examined.

2.5.4
Graph and skeleton-based methods
Human pose has a natural graph structure.
Joints form nodes and anatomical con-
nections form edges. Across time, corresponding joints can also be connected between
consecutive frames. Graph convolutional networks exploit this structure rather than
treating the skeleton as an unordered vector.
Pedestrian Graph and Pedestrian Graph+ used graph-based processing of pose and
related features for crossing prediction [25], [39].
TrouSPI-Net modeled skeletal se-
quences through parallel temporal processing and attention [24].
PedAST-GCN in-
troduced spatial, temporal, and channel attention in a graph-convolutional framework
[26]. GTransPDM combined a graph-embedded transformer with positional decoupling
and ego-vehicle motion [27]. GATEPose subsequently fused ViTPose skeletons, bound-
ing boxes, and explicitly computed orientation angles, but evaluated custom JAADpose
and PIEpose collections rather than the common benchmark subset [28]. These methods
can capture coordinated motion across body parts while preserving anatomical topology,
although their inputs and evaluation populations are not interchangeable.
Graph models reduce some of the arbitrary structure imposed by flattening keypoints,
but their learned representations are still latent. A graph activation may be associated
with the lower body or a temporal transition, yet it does not automatically translate
into a statement such as “the lead foot rose relative to the hips” or “the stride widened.”
Graph methods and interpretable geometric cues therefore answer different questions.
Graph networks seek a flexible representation that maximizes predictive performance.
Named geometric cues trade some representational freedom for direct inspectability.
The comparison is not necessarily either-or. A graph model can use engineered edge
features, and a tabular model can combine named cues with raw keypoints. The impor-
tant experimental question is whether the explicit cues add information that the chosen
baseline does not already capture.
2.5.5
Scene graphs, trajectory fusion, and joint prediction
Some methods make the relations between the pedestrian and surrounding traffic objects
explicit. Traffic-aware scene-graph models represent objects and their interactions rather
than relying only on a global image embedding [40]. This is attractive because cross-
ing decisions are relational: a traffic signal, approaching vehicle, curb, or neighboring
pedestrian matters through its relation to the target pedestrian.
Another direction couples intention with trajectory or future action. Yao et al. modeled
intent and semantic action jointly, using predicted future actions to inform present

Chapter 2 Background and Related Work
22
intent detection and an attentive relation network to encode environmental relations [41].
PTINet jointly predicts intention and future trajectory using local pedestrian attributes,
global visual features, optical flow, and past positions [42]. Trajectory models such as
Holistic LSTM explicitly model interactions between pedestrian intention, vehicle speed,
and global dynamics [20]. Mutable intention filtering and Warp LSTM approach long-
term trajectory prediction by maintaining multiple intention hypotheses and correcting
a nominal trajectory [43].
These methods are adjacent to, rather than identical with, the binary anticipation task
used in this thesis. Joint prediction can provide richer behavioral modeling, but it also
changes the targets, losses, and required annotations. Trajectory error cannot be directly
compared with crossing F1, and current intent detection cannot be directly compared
with a fixed pre-event TTE benchmark.
TrajFusionNet represents a more recent attempt to combine observed and predicted
trajectory information with visual representations for crossing prediction [14]. At the
time of this audit it is a preprint, so it is used as current context rather than a peer-
reviewed SOTA anchor. Its relevance to this thesis lies in the strength of trajectory-only
or trajectory-centered models. When box dynamics already provide a strong signal, pose
must contribute something beyond a second encoding of the same motion.
2.5.6
Vision-language approaches
Vision–language models (VLMs) have begun to extend pedestrian-intention prediction
beyond conventional visual classification, but language can enter the pipeline in different
ways. ClipCross is a discriminative use of a vision–language representation: it optimizes
CLIP text embeddings, combines temporal image embeddings, and uses the resulting
cross-modal features in a binary classifier rather than generating free-form language
[44]. PedVLM instead encodes RGB observations and optical flow with a CLIP-based
vision encoder and combines them with question–answer prompts in a T5 language
model.
It predicts the crossing class while also producing a textual account of the
observed behavior; its accompanying PedPrompt resource supplies prompt–answer pairs
derived from established pedestrian datasets [16]. LLaMAPed applies VideoLLaMA2 to
video observations and prompts to predict crossing intention on JAAD without task-
specific training, i.e., in a zero-shot setting [45]. More recently, VLMPed-CoT has used
multimodal input, chain-of-thought supervision, data distillation, and two-stage fine-
tuning on a joint JAAD/PIE corpus, while also examining the relationship between
input configuration and inference time [46].
These methods illustrate discriminative
language-aligned representation, supervised language generation, zero-shot prompting,

Chapter 2 Background and Related Work
23
and fine-tuned reasoning rather than directly comparable variants of one benchmark
protocol.
Generative VLMs offer capabilities that a binary classifier does not. They can express
a decision in natural language, refer to several scene elements in one response, and
potentially exploit broad visual knowledge acquired during pretraining. These capa-
bilities make them a powerful direction for combining prediction with a human-facing
explanation interface.
Several questions specific to generative VLM use nevertheless remain to be addressed
systematically in future pedestrian-intention research.
A generated rationale can be
fluent and persuasive without faithfully reporting the visual evidence that produced
the prediction. Outputs can also change with prompt wording, decoding settings, and
model-version updates, which complicates reproducibility. Because responses are free-
form, a study must define how they are converted into a consistent crossing/non-crossing
decision; otherwise, formatting and interpretation choices become part of the evaluation.
Generating language also introduces an additional decoding stage after visual processing,
with latency that can depend on response length. Its suitability for time-critical embed-
ded inference must therefore be demonstrated rather than assumed. Future work should
develop standardized prompts and output schemas, controlled or repeated decoding
protocols, explanation-faithfulness and visual-grounding tests, and complete reporting
of memory and end-to-end latency. Recent work has begun to examine some of these
dimensions, including inference-time sensitivity [46], but they are not yet addressed
through a common evaluation standard. These open issues do not diminish the poten-
tial of generative VLMs, but they currently limit how confidently their explanations,
reproducibility, and real-time readiness can be assessed.
For the present thesis, vision–language approaches are therefore relevant as an adjacent
interpretability direction rather than as a direct experimental baseline. Discriminative
variants such as ClipCross learn language-aligned semantic features, whereas generative
variants can expose decisions through natural-language responses. The narrower ques-
tion pursued here is whether part of the evidence supplied to a classifier can itself consist
of named, fixed, and inspectable geometric measurements. The conceptual foundations
of that representation are discussed below; its equations are defined in Chapter 3.

2.6
Human Pose Estimation and Pose Representation
2.6.1
Two-dimensional pose estimation
Most pedestrian-intention datasets do not provide manually annotated anatomical joints.
Pose must therefore be inferred from images.
In a top-down pipeline, a pedestrian
detector or an annotated bounding box first defines the person crop. A pose estimator
then predicts a set of joint coordinates and confidence values inside that crop.

Chapter 2 Background and Related Work
25
A common representation follows the COCO convention of 17 keypoints: nose, eyes, ears,
shoulders, elbows, wrists, hips, knees, and ankles [48]. Each keypoint is represented by
an image-plane coordinate and, in many systems, a confidence score.
The resulting
skeleton contains enough structure to estimate basic head, torso, arm, and leg geometry.
Early intention studies used OpenPose or related convolutional estimators [49]. Later
work adopted more accurate architectures, including transformer-based pose estimation.
ViTPose provides a simple vision-transformer framework for top-down human pose es-
timation and is used as the upstream pose extractor in this thesis [7]. The use of a
stronger pose estimator may improve keypoint stability, but it does not remove the
basic limitations of two-dimensional pose in driving footage.
2.6.2
Raw keypoints as model input
The simplest pose representation concatenates joint coordinates into a vector for each
frame. A temporal model then receives a sequence of these vectors. Coordinates may
be expressed in the full image, relative to the pedestrian box, or normalized by body
scale. Confidence values can be included as additional dimensions.
Raw coordinates preserve detailed spatial information, but they also contain nuisance
variation. The same posture appears at different image positions and scales. Camera
perspective changes the apparent geometry. Left-right orientation may be ambiguous,
and a mirrored pedestrian can produce a very different vector. If coordinates are not
normalized carefully, the classifier may learn scene position more strongly than body
configuration.
Flattening an entire 16-frame sequence also creates a high-dimensional input. Missing
or low-confidence joints must be handled through zeros, interpolation, masking, or im-
putation. Each strategy changes what the model can learn. A zero may be interpreted
as a valid location unless a mask is supplied. Interpolation preserves temporal length
but can create smooth motion that was never observed.
These issues do not make raw keypoints unusable. They make the representation sensi-
tive to preprocessing and model capacity. Raw keypoints are valuable as a comparison
because they contain more detailed pose information than a small engineered set. If
a compact representation performs similarly or complements them, the result suggests
that meaningful structure can be retained without requiring every coordinate.

Chapter 2 Background and Related Work
26
2.6.3
Skeleton graphs and learned embeddings
Graph-based approaches encode anatomical connectivity explicitly. Spatial edges con-
nect neighboring joints, while temporal edges connect the same joint across frames. A
graph convolution can therefore learn coordinated patterns such as leg motion or shoul-
der rotation.
Other methods transform the skeleton into heatmaps, rasterized lines, or overlays on
the pedestrian crop. This allows convolutional networks to process pose and appearance
together. Recurrent methods encode the keypoint sequence into a hidden state, while
transformers treat joints, frames, or modalities as tokens.
Graph, heatmap, and learned-embedding representations leave much of the interpreta-
tion of the skeleton to the model. Unlike an explicitly calculated feature such as ankle
distance or torso angle, the network can combine joints and frames in relationships that
were not specified in advance. This flexibility may capture useful patterns that a manu-
ally designed feature set would omit. However, the resulting hidden features are difficult
to interpret because an embedding dimension has no predetermined physical meaning. A
saliency map may show that the ankles or shoulders influenced the prediction, but it does
not reveal whether the model used stride width, foot elevation, body rotation, or some
other relationship. This distinction concerns representation-level meaning rather than
complete model transparency: post-hoc saliency can identify influential inputs without
assigning a fixed physical meaning to every learned feature.
2.6.4
Pose quality and robustness in driving scenes
Onboard traffic footage presents pose estimators with challenging but practically rel-
evant conditions. Pedestrians may occupy only a small image region, particularly at
the earlier TTEs that matter for anticipation, while occlusion, motion blur, clothing,
carried objects, groups, and unusual viewpoints can reduce the visibility of individual
joints. Transformer-based estimators such as ViTPose provide a stronger modern alter-
native to earlier keypoint extractors [7]. For crossing prediction, however, useful pose
does not require every joint to be reconstructed perfectly in every frame. The objective
is to obtain sufficiently consistent evidence about visible posture and motion over the
observation window.
In a top-down pipeline, pose quality also depends on the pedestrian crop and the con-
sistency of the associated track. A box that excludes a foot or includes part of another
person can affect the estimated skeleton. Confidence scores make such uncertainty ob-
servable instead of leaving it hidden inside the preprocessing pipeline. Box-relative or

Chapter 2 Background and Related Work
27
body-scale normalization, explicit missingness handling, valid-frame counts, and tem-
poral aggregation provide practical ways to reduce sensitivity to scale variation and
isolated estimation failures. These procedures do not remove all pose noise, but they
make its treatment explicit and auditable.
Two-dimensional joints describe camera-relative rather than full three-dimensional ge-
ometry. This limits the interpretation that can be attached to a measurement, but it
does not remove its usefulness. Relative joint positions can still describe visible image-
plane patterns associated with stride, foot placement, torso configuration, and body
orientation. A head-orientation cue inferred from facial keypoints should therefore be
described as a camera-relative orientation proxy rather than a direct measurement of
gaze. Precise cue definitions preserve the value of the observation without assigning it
a stronger meaning than the image supports.
Pose-derived cues share the quality of their source keypoints. For example, an inaccurate
ankle can affect both stride and foot-elevation measurements. With explicitly defined
cues, however, this dependency can be traced to the contributing joints, recorded through
quality fields, and examined alongside the resulting prediction. Comparisons with raw
keypoints and bounding-box dynamics can then test whether the derived representa-
tion retains robust or complementary information rather than assuming that usefulness
follows from interpretability alone.
A large benchmark combining reliable ground-truth anatomical pose with event-aligned
crossing intention is still unavailable under the established PIE/JAAD prediction pro-
tocol. New resources emphasize situated intention, explanations, or pose estimated over
expanded dataset portions, but their tasks and sampling are not directly interchangeable
with that benchmark [11], [28]. Direct pose-fidelity analysis and downstream crossing
evaluation therefore answer complementary questions: the former examines the quality
of the estimated skeleton, while the latter determines whether the resulting representa-
tion supports the intended prediction task.
2.6.5
From coordinates to behavioral geometry
Explicit pose geometry has a substantial research lineage. Fang, Vazquez, and L´opez
investigated engineered pose features for onboard intention detection [22]. Fang and
L´opez’s subsequent JAAD study selected nine keypoints and calculated normalized co-
ordinate differences, pairwise distances, pairwise angles, and triangle angles. It concate-
nated 396 features per frame across the observation window and used a random forest
for classification [23]. This is a direct methodological foundation: geometric relation-
ships are calculated before the classifier learns a decision function. Ankle separation and

Chapter 2 Background and Related Work
28
shoulder-line geometry share primitives with the measurements developed here. Their
presence in earlier work must be acknowledged even when the selected joints, normal-
ization, and temporal representation differ.
Explicit geometry also appears inside neural architectures. TrouSPI-Net calculates Joint
Collection Distances (JCD), comprising pairwise Euclidean joint distances, and processes
their temporal sequence with recurrent encoders alongside a skeletal stream [24]. The
distances are deterministic inputs; their temporal encoding is learned. Sidharta et al.
use temporal angular 2D skeletal pose for predicting the crossing behavior of small
pedestrian groups [50]. This establishes an angular and temporal representation prece-
dent, although its group-level task differs from individual event-aligned prediction. The
available description supports that relationship without establishing that any particular
angle equals a thesis cue.
Two related approaches emphasize coordinate transformations and relative motion.
Faster-PCPNet combines pose, bounding boxes, ego-speed, and a triple quasi-polar co-
ordinate representation with graph-based learning [51]. Its stated motivation includes
pedestrian–vehicle relative motion.
GTransPDM explicitly transforms position using
reference-relative displacement and bounding-box area change before combining it with
a learned pose-graph representation [27]. These methods support the importance of ref-
erence frames and separating apparent motion components. In particular, GTransPDM’s
position transformation should not be described as an anatomical joint-angle measure-
ment.
GATEPose provides a further example of explicit geometry alongside latent pose en-
coding: it combines a calculated orientation-angle stream with ViTPose skeletons and
bounding boxes in a neural temporal architecture [28]. The conceptual overlap is ex-
plicit orientation evidence. A claim that its angle vector reproduces a particular thesis
equation would require verification of the contributing joints, reference axes, normaliza-
tion, and temporal operations. Its custom pose collections also prevent an automatic
numerical comparison with the standard benchmark.
2.6.6
Named measurements and the limited FACS analogy
The Facial Action Coding System (FACS) provides a separate conceptual foundation.
It describes visible facial movement through anatomically grounded Action Units [52].
Ekman and Friesen’s measurement work distinguishes visible facial movement from the
information an observer infers from it [6]. The relevant principle is to separate a de-
fined observable movement from an interpretation attached to it. The analogy does not

Chapter 2 Background and Related Work
29
establish a facial-muscle basis for whole-body intention, nor does it validate a crossing
label from any single movement.
This thesis uses the term Behavioral Intention Units (BIUs) for its explicitly named,
mathematically defined pose-derived measurements. The term describes the proposed
crossing-oriented representation; it is not attributed to the preceding papers as an equiva-
lent established coding system. The preceding discussion distinguishes shared geometric
primitives, related coordinate transformations, and learned representations rather than
treating them as interchangeable definitions.
Chapter 3 develops the BIU definitions, normalization and temporal summaries. Their
relationship to the preceding work rests on how crossing-oriented measurements are
selected and organized, with empirical comparisons used to assess that organization.
2.7
Evaluation and Benchmark Comparability
2.7.1
Evaluation practices in previous work
Published pedestrian-intention studies report different combinations of accuracy, pre-
cision, recall, F1-score, and area-under-curve measures. The benchmark analysis also
showed that performance changes with the temporal protocol and standardized the eval-
uation setting so that model scores could be interpreted under matched observation and
TTE conditions [3]. Published results provide a useful reference when interpreted using
the dataset and temporal settings reported by each study. Where additional protocol de-
tails are unavailable, the results can still be included as reported literature comparisons,
with those uncertainties acknowledged.
Threshold-dependent measures such as precision, recall, F1-score, and accuracy describe
performance at a selected operating point. The area under the receiver operating char-
acteristic curve (ROC-AUC) and the area under the precision–recall curve (PR-AUC)
instead summarize ranking behavior across thresholds; PR-AUC is particularly sensitive
to class prevalence. The formal definitions and the exact evaluation measures adopted
in this thesis are specified in Chapter 3.
2.7.2
Model and threshold selection
A fair evaluation separates training, validation, and test data. Hyperparameters, model
family, feature configuration, and any decision threshold should be selected using training
and validation data. The test set should be evaluated only after these choices are fixed.

Chapter 2 Background and Related Work
30
This principle is particularly important when several classifiers and feature groups are
compared. Selecting the best row after inspecting test F1 turns the test set into an
additional validation set and makes the reported performance optimistic.
The same
applies to choosing a threshold that maximizes test F1.
2.7.3
Dependence among windows
Sliding windows generated from one track are correlated. They share frames and the
same underlying event. Standard statistical tests that treat every window as independent
underestimate uncertainty.
A more appropriate unit for resampling is the pedestrian track. In a track-level clus-
ter bootstrap, tracks are sampled with replacement and all their windows are included
together. This preserves within-track dependence while estimating how a metric differ-
ence varies across the observed pedestrian population. Repeated training seeds provide
a separate check on sensitivity to model initialization or stochastic training.
The distinction can materially change the interpretation of a result. A small improve-
ment may appear highly significant at window level because thousands of overlapping
samples are counted separately, yet become uncertain when evaluated over a few hun-
dred tracks. Statistical analysis should match the structure by which the data were
generated.
2.8
Research Gap and Thesis Positioning
The reviewed approaches leave questions about the form, timing and interpretation of
pose evidence, despite substantial progress in multimodal prediction.
First, raw keypoint sequences, learned skeleton embeddings, and pose overlays offer
different ways to represent body configuration. These representations may support good
predictive performance, but they do not by themselves identify the visible body behavior
associated with a decision. A model can rely on a learned pose feature without revealing
whether the relevant pattern was stride, torso orientation, foot movement, or another
correlation.
Second, the engineered-pose precedents leave open which selection and organization of
geometric measurements is useful for crossing anticipation under a common event-aligned
protocol.

Chapter 2 Background and Related Work
31
Third, adding pose to a model is not automatically beneficial. Pose quality varies with
scale and occlusion, and a high-dimensional keypoint block can add noise to an already
strong trajectory baseline. The useful question is therefore conditional: under which
dataset and TTE conditions does pose provide complementary signal?
Fourth, many comparisons treat overlapping windows as independent and focus on a sin-
gle test score. A representation intended for safety-related prediction should be assessed
with track-aware uncertainty, controlled model selection, and clear separation between
main benchmark results and diagnostic analyses.
These gaps motivate the BIU representation and multimodal XGBoost architecture de-
veloped in Chapter 3. Matched raw-pose and BIU controls, including adapted PedCMT
[5] and standardized MSLSTM [4], test representation complementarity across predic-
tors.

3.1
Research Design and Evidence Hierarchy
The research follows a representation-centered experimental design.
Given the same
pedestrian observation windows, models are trained using different feature families. A
comparison between two feature families is considered controlled only when the samples,
labels, splits, classifier family, selection rule, and evaluation rule are held fixed. Fusion
experiments concatenate feature groups on the same sequence identifiers, which makes
it possible to assess their predictive contribution without changing the cohort. BIUs
reorganize their source measurements; a gain over raw-pose controls indicates that the
predictor used that representation more effectively, not that the transformation created
new sensory information.
The evidence is organized into five categories:
1. Main benchmark evidence uses a frozen train–validation–test protocol and is
eligible to support the central performance claims.
2. Diagnostic evidence investigates mechanisms such as direction handling, nor-
malization, pose confidence, or calibration. It can explain a result but does not
silently replace the main benchmark.
3. Qualitative evidence examines selected tracks, keypoint traces, and error cases.
It illustrates model behavior but is not a population-level performance estimate.
4. Negative or no-advance evidence records hypotheses that did not produce
a reliable improvement.
Such findings constrain the thesis claims and are not
removed simply because they are unfavorable.
5. Superseded, invalid, or incomplete evidence includes protocol errors, non-
comparable task settings, interrupted runs, and proposals without a completed
evaluation.
These artifacts are retained for provenance but are excluded from
headline conclusions.
This hierarchy is particularly important for JAAD, where alternative pedestrian popu-
lations and event constructions can materially change the task, and for threshold analy-
ses, where a threshold selected using validation data answers a different operating-point
question from a fixed threshold.

3.4
Pose Extraction and Sequence Alignment
The BIU pipeline assumes that pedestrian detection or annotation boxes and two-
dimensional pose are supplied by an upstream perception stage. Pose is represented
in the 17-joint COCO order: nose; left and right eyes; ears; shoulders; elbows; wrists;
hips; knees; and ankles. Each frame record contains full-image (x, y) coordinates and,
where available, 17 keypoint confidences and a skeleton confidence.
The historical BIU keypoint cache is documented as a top-down ViTPose-S lineage using
annotated pedestrian boxes and a COCO configuration with a 256 × 192 pose input
[7].
Two preserved PIE copies are byte-identical and contain 740,901 unique frame
records. The original historical checkpoint and execution log are no longer available,
however, so the original PIE checkpoint identity cannot be cryptographically verified.
The thesis therefore describes this source as a verified cache with ViTPose-S lineage
rather than claiming a fully reproducible re-extraction. Later JAAD cache extensions
use the corresponding pinned lineage, but the exact source manifest for each experiment
remains authoritative.
Sequence alignment is performed by identity rather than row position.
PIE records
are indexed by (set, video, pedestrian, frame) and JAAD records by (video,
pedestrian, frame). Every main benchmark window must recover its exact ordered set
of 16 consecutive frame records. Feature tables are joined using a unique sequence id,
and validators check row counts, identifier equality, label equality, duplicate identifiers,
and entirely missing feature columns. Missing frames are reported rather than silently
interpolated or replaced.
The use of annotation boxes isolates the representation question from detector failures
but is not a complete deployment evaluation.
The downstream BIU computation is
automatic once boxes and pose are available; nevertheless, Chapter 4 distinguishes this
decision-stage cost from the cost and error of upstream detection and pose estimation.

3.6
Comparison and Fusion Representations
Four information families are compared under matched samples:
1. BBox describes image-plane location, size, and dynamics. In the frozen main PIE
schema, center x/y, width, height, area, and aspect ratio are aggregated across the
observation, then combined with velocity, displacement, size-change, scale-change,
and validity fields. The resulting block contains 53 features.
2. KP preserves the estimated pose with minimal engineering. The published BIU
v1 comparison flattens (x, y, confidence) for 17 COCO joints over 16 frames, pro-
ducing 16 × 17 × 3 = 816 values. It is less compact and less directly interpretable
than BIUs, but it tests whether manual geometric summaries discard useful pose
information.
3. BIU is either the 72-feature v1 block or the experiment-declared v2 block. BIU v1
emphasizes anatomical posture and gait summaries; BIU v2 adds direction-aware
movement and uses a more selective summary policy.

4. Fusion concatenates blocks after a one-to-one join on sequence id. The frozen
PIE v1+BBox block has 125 features, while the Experiment 012 v2-core+BBox

For a box (x1, y1, x2, y2), define center (cx, cy), width w = x2 −x1, height h = y2 −y1,

and height, the last-to-first area ratio, and the valid-box count. These BBox53 features

from the zero-prepended difference convention in the v2 extractor.

appended to those neural raw-pose streams. Both controls are identified explicitly when
results are compared.

3.7.2
Why use trees with the engineered representation?
The BIU and BBox features expose quantities such as orientation, displacement, scale
change and early-to-late motion before classifier training. A tree can therefore condi-
tion directly on one measured cue and then refine the decision using another, without
first learning those measurements from raw coordinate sequences. This is a plausible
advantage of the representation for a tree predictor: meaningful feature axes and non-
linear threshold interactions fit its decision structure. General tabular-learning evidence
motivates this rationale [34]; its applicability here is an empirical question.
The same feature construction can also help a neural predictor. The MSLSTM BBox con-
trol replaces a raw 16×4 coordinate sequence and its recurrent branch with BBox53 and
a vector projector, while holding the other streams, normalized fusion, cohort and train-
ing recipe fixed. This comparison tests an engineered representation together with its
receiving encoder. It does not isolate a feature-only effect or directly compare XGBoost
with MSLSTM. Its outcome belongs in Chapter 4, alongside the matched pose/BIU
controls and the separately identified comparisons between predictors.
Handcrafted summaries may discard useful temporal detail, and neural encoders may
exploit patterns they omit. Training-set size, optimization, model capacity and input
availability can also affect relative performance. Consequently, a favorable XGBoost
result would support the tested architecture and training conditions; the BBox control
alone cannot establish its cause or a general superiority over neural prediction.

3.8.3
Why constrain the BIU correction?
After the context-token trial, a direct-fusion trial kept the trained BBox/ego baseline
frozen and learned a signed BIU correction to its crossing score. This made the added
branch’s effect explicit.
Validation diagnostics showed that full-strength corrections
could improve probability ranking while overturning correct baseline decisions. Remov-
ing training duplication did not resolve this behavior; a separate validation intercept
diagnostic also failed to recover the baseline’s classification performance. These obser-
vations motivated limiting the correction’s magnitude.
The problem is excessive influence of a sample-dependent correction, rather than BIUs
literally receiving all credit or being intrinsically misleading. The evidence comes from
saved validation probabilities and decision transitions in the direct-fusion trials; it is not
an attribution analysis of the earlier context-token model. Those trials remain design
history here, rather than additional test-result tables in Chapter 4.

probabilities. Ready-feature prediction includes median imputation and XGBoost: 200

Chapter 4
Results and Discussion
This chapter examines how the BIU representation behaves with lightweight classifiers,
how it contributes to the multimodal XGBoost architecture, and whether its value ex-
tends to the adapted neural predictors. The analysis follows the questions raised during
development: whether engineered pose summaries complement bounding-box dynamics,
whether direction-aware refinements improve those summaries, and what is gained or
lost when additional measurements and context are combined. Cue-level explanations
then examine the evidence used by the fitted predictors.
4.1
Evaluation Scope
The primary results use PIE with 16 consecutive observed frames and a time-to-event
of 30–60 frames. The training, validation and test sets contain 2,366, 3,619 and 3,760
windows, respectively; the test set contains 1,005 crossing and 2,755 noncrossing win-
dows. The split and window construction follow Section 3.3. PR-AUC denotes average
precision throughout this chapter.
Descriptive model names are used throughout the results.
Appendix A maps these
names to repository experiment identifiers and source artifacts for reproducibility.

The supported benefit is diagnostic: explicit measurements make it possible to examine
where model reliance is helpful, mixed or adverse relative to the labels. Demonstrating
adaptation would require a controlled change developed on training/validation data and
evaluated independently. The test explanations presented here are not used to choose
such a change.

What the behavioral cues contribute.
Figure 4.6 explains the saved PSI-native
BBox+v1 model, not the v2 or extended models. All ten behavioral v1 cues are dis-
played, excluding quality counters. This separates explanation of a fitted predictor from
a claim that the same cue has the same effect in every architecture.
Stride length pushes toward the label in 63.0% of test windows, while normalized shoul-
der width does so in 45.7%. Yet shoulder width has a small positive average aligned
contribution, approximately 0.0014 log-odds. Less frequent, larger supportive contribu-

seeds, with a sample standard deviation of approximately 0.0054. Neither its sign nor

Class-specific inspection provides further detail. Shoulder width points toward the label
in approximately 37.9% of positive-label windows and 69.5% of negative-label windows.

dominate the score. Contribution direction must therefore remain distinct from predic-

4.10
Limitations of the Current Evidence
The evidence supports the representation and architecture conditionally. A geometric
transformation can make pose easier for a particular learner to use, but does not ensure
improvement for another classifier, input package or population. The additional neural
ablations test their own input and fusion settings; their results do not automatically
transfer to XGBoost.
The measurements also inherit errors and ambiguity from two-dimensional pose. Ap-
parent orientation, shoulder width and foot displacement can vary with viewpoint and

Chapter 4 Results and Discussion
85
visibility as well as pedestrian behavior. Named cues expose these assumptions for in-
spection, but do not remove them. Attribution decomposes the fitted model’s score and
is not proof of the pedestrian’s reasoning or of a causal effect on crossing.
Finally, repeated cohort exposure, overlapping windows and differences between tasks
limit the breadth of generalization claims. The absence of a retained 56-D zero-shot eval-
uation, the absence of independent evaluation of cue-based adaptation, and differences
in literature protocols and hardware remain boundaries of the evidence. They do not
prevent reporting the supported PIE controls, the measured operating-point trade-offs
or the demonstrated ability to inspect cue contributions.
